DISCUSSION BOARD INITIAL POST – Kevin Harrison

One creative change I would like to see in church ministry is the integration of digital discipleship platforms to complement in-person gatherings. In an age where technology permeates every aspect of daily life, many churches have been slow to adopt digital tools for spiritual formation. This change is not about replacing face-to-face community but about extending discipleship beyond the four walls of the church building.

The reason I want to see this change is rooted in the reality of how people, especially younger generations, engage with information and community. Social media, podcasts, and online courses have become primary learning tools for many. Churches that fail to meet people where they are—online—risk becoming irrelevant to those who could benefit most from their message. As James Emery White argues in Rethinking the Church, the church must adapt its methods while remaining faithful to its message (White 45). Digital discipleship is not a compromise of the gospel; it is a creative means of delivering it to a wider audience.

The positive impact of this change on church ministry would be multifaceted. First, it would allow churches to reach people who are unable to attend in-person services due to work, health, or geographic limitations. A single mother working Sundays could still participate in a Bible study via a mobile app. A college student away from home could stay connected to their home church through live streaming and online small groups. Second, digital platforms provide opportunities for deeper discipleship through on-demand content, discussion forums, and personalized learning paths. Third, it creates a lower barrier for seekers who may be hesitant to visit a church in person but are willing to engage with content online.

Critics may argue that digital ministry lacks the authenticity of face-to-face community. While this concern is valid, it misses the point. Digital discipleship is not a replacement for in-person gathering; it is a supplement. Just as Paul used letters to disciple churches he could not visit in person, modern churches can use technology to disciple those they cannot reach physically. The early church adapted to the tools available—roads, letters, synagogues—and the modern church must do the same with digital tools.

In conclusion, integrating digital discipleship platforms is a creative and necessary change that would allow churches to extend their reach, deepen discipleship, and meet people where they are. This change aligns with the Great Commission's call to go into all the world—even the digital world—and make disciples of all nations.

Works Cited
